# Business Plan Writing Style Rulebook

A quick-reference guide for writing clear, credible, investor-ready business plans.

---

## 1. Structure

**1.1 — Lead with a standalone executive summary.**
Many readers (investors, lenders) only read this page. It must make sense with nothing else read.

**1.2 — One idea per paragraph, one topic per section.**
If a paragraph covers two ideas, split it.

**1.3 — Use headers and subheaders liberally.**
A skimming reader should be able to navigate by headers alone.

---

## 2. Tone

**2.1 — Plain language over jargon.**
Avoid "synergy," "disruptive," "revolutionary," "best-in-class," "world-class" unless backed by a specific fact.

**2.2 — Confident, not hype-y.**
State what you will do, not how amazing it will be. Let facts carry the excitement.

**2.3 — Specific beats vague.**
"We project $2.3M in Year 2 revenue, driven by 400 enterprise accounts at $5,750 ACV" beats "We expect strong revenue growth."

**2.4 — Active voice, always.**
"We will launch in Q2" — not "It is anticipated that launch will occur in Q2."

**2.5 — Default to semi-formal, not stiff or casual.**
A business plan sits between an academic paper and marketing copy. Fully formal ("Management anticipates that...") reads outdated; casual ("We're gonna crush this market") reads unserious. Aim for the register of a well-written memo.

**2.6 — Match formality to the specific reader.**
- Banks, government grant bodies, institutional lenders → most formal: no contractions, no humor, precise and impersonal
- VC / angel investors → semi-formal: contractions are fine ("we're," "don't"), direct and confident, still professional
- Internal strategic plan (for a board or leadership team) → can run more conversational

**2.7 — No humor, idioms, or slang, at any formality level.**
Jokes and colloquialisms ("home run," "no-brainer") read as filler or don't translate for non-native readers. Cut them regardless of audience.

**2.8 — Skip exclamation marks and rhetorical questions.**
"This is a $10B opportunity." not "This is a $10B opportunity!" Let the number carry the energy.

**2.9 — Reserve "you" for the direct ask.**
The plan is about "we" and "the market" throughout. Switch to addressing the reader as "you" only in the funding request or call to action, where it should feel direct.

**2.10 — "I" only for solo founders, and use sparingly even then.**
A single-founder plan can use "I" in the founder bio or vision section, but should still default to "we" when describing the company's actions.

---

## 3. Numbers & Evidence

**3.1 — Every claim needs a source or a stated assumption.**
"Based on [source/logic], we project X." Never present a number as self-evident.

**3.2 — No hockey sticks without logic.**
If revenue triples in Year 2, the text must explain the specific driver (new channel, price change, headcount added).

**3.3 — Show the math, briefly.**
One line of "how we got this number" builds more trust than the number alone.

**3.4 — Cite competitors and market data by name.**
"The market" is not a source. Name the report, the competitor, the data point.

---

## 4. Formatting

**4.1 — Short paragraphs (3–5 sentences max).**
Break up anything longer.

**4.2 — Bullets for lists, tables for comparisons and numbers.**
Never describe a comparison in prose that a table would show in half the space.

**4.3 — Consistent formatting throughout.**
Same header styles, same table format, same number formatting (e.g., always $1,000 or always $1K — don't mix).

**4.4 — Charts over paragraphs for financial data.**
A simple revenue chart replaces a paragraph of numbers.

---

## 5. Voice & Consistency

**5.1 — Pick one point of view and hold it.**
Use "we" throughout (most common for business plans). Don't drift into "I" or "the company" mid-document.

**5.2 — Consistent tense.**
Present tense for current facts, future tense for projections — don't mix within a section.

**5.3 — Consistent terminology.**
If you call it "the platform" on page 2, don't call it "the app" on page 5.

---

## 6. Length & Editing

**6.1 — As short as possible while complete.**
Length is not a virtue. Cut anything that doesn't inform a decision.

**6.2 — Write for skimming, edit for reading.**
Draft to get ideas down; edit ruthlessly so key facts are findable in seconds. Investors spend under four minutes on a first read (DocSend, 200,000+ deck views) — every section needs to land in 10–15 seconds for a skimming reader.

**6.3 — Cut qualifiers and hedging.**
Delete "very," "really," "we believe," "we think," "quite," "so," "good," "unique" where the claim can just be stated. "Very fast growth" means something different to every reader; a stated percentage means the same thing to everyone.

**6.4 — Read it as your harshest investor would.**
Every unsupported claim is a place they'll push back — find it before they do.

---

## 7. Investor Persuasion

Grounded in Sequoia Capital's pitch framework, Y Combinator's fundraising guidance, Guy Kawasaki's 10/20/30 rule, teardown analyses of Airbnb's original 2008 seed deck, and DocSend's data from 200,000+ investor deck views. Sources listed at the end of this section.

**7.1 — Lead with your strongest fact, not the expected order.**
Don't default to intro → problem → solution if something else is more compelling. Y Combinator's fundraising guidance flags leading with a standard format instead of your strongest point as founders' most common structural mistake.

**7.2 — Pass the "anyone can understand it" test.**
State what the company does in one plain sentence with zero industry jargon. Airbnb's earliest pitch material described the company as letting people book rooms with locals instead of hotels — understandable in three seconds, no explanation needed.

**7.3 — Show the proof, don't describe that it exists.**
State the concrete number or artifact rather than asserting a quality. Not "the platform is live and gaining traction" — the actual booking count, user count, or revenue figure. Teardowns of Airbnb's original deck point to this exact substitution as a key reason it worked.

**7.4 — Avoid the phrases that read as inexperience.**
A handful of phrases are documented tells that a founder hasn't done the work:
- Citing total addressable market and "just 1%" of it, instead of a bottoms-up estimate of who you can realistically reach
- Claiming "no competitors" — every real problem has alternatives, even doing nothing
- Calling growth "viral" without naming the mechanism driving it
- Framing the ask around "runway" instead of what the money will build or prove
- Volunteering an "exit strategy" in the narrative, which shifts attention to risk instead of opportunity

**7.5 — State the business model in one sentence.**
How the company makes money should read as a single, complete sentence — not a list of "potential revenue streams." Analyses of Airbnb's deck repeatedly single out its one-line business model statement (a flat commission per booking) as one of its strongest slides, precisely because it needed no further explanation.

**7.6 — Build a three-beat narrative: problem, turn, proof.**
Structure persuasive sections as: the problem in the customer's own words → the moment the solution appears → evidence it's already working. This is the core of Guy Kawasaki's storytelling guidance and shows up across most pitch deck teardowns as the difference between a narrative and a data dump.

**7.7 — Give the reader a specific decision to make.**
State the exact ask tied to a specific, falsifiable outcome, not an open-ended funding goal. Pattern: "We're raising $[amount] to go from [current metric] to [target metric] by [date]." A specific ask forces a real yes-or-no decision, per YC's Michael Seibel — more likely to produce a check than a vague one that's easy to defer.

**7.8 — Back growth claims with a credible "why now."**
A large market size alone isn't a claim — pair it with a stated reason the timing is right. Sequoia's founder guide calls out an unexplained market-size claim as one of the fastest ways to lose credibility with an experienced investor.

**7.9 — Keep the narrative positive; let the reader raise the risk.**
Don't pre-emptively narrate downside scenarios (runway, worst-case exits) in the main text — this shifts investor attention toward risk instead of opportunity. Address downside if asked; don't volunteer it in writing.

**7.10 — Put your sharpest editing into business model, traction, and team.**
DocSend's data across 200,000+ deck views shows these three sections get disproportionately more investor attention than product or vision — edit them hardest.
