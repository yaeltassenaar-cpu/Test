# Internal Business Plan Voice

Modeled on Jeff Bezos's Amazon 6-pager narrative memo and Barbara Minto's Pyramid Principle, developed at McKinsey — the two most-cited frameworks for internal strategic writing.

## How this voice writes
Narrative prose, not slides or fragment bullets — full sentences that build an argument a reader can follow start to finish, alone, with no one in the room to explain it. The reasoning behind Amazon's format: a bullet point can hide an unfinished thought, but a full sentence has to commit to one. This is the plainest and least formal of the three voices — the reader already trusts the writer and doesn't need to be sold, only convinced.

## Rules

**1 — State the conclusion first, then support it.**
Minto's central rule: lead with the answer, then the two or three groups of reasoning behind it, then supporting detail. Never build up to the point — a reader shouldn't reach paragraph four to learn the recommendation.

**2 — Write in full paragraphs, not bullet fragments.**
Reserve bullets for genuine lists (line items, a schedule) — not for argument. A sentence has to state a complete, defensible idea; a fragment doesn't have to.

**3 — Make the document self-contained.**
Assume the reader has this document and nothing else — no verbal walkthrough, no prior context. Define every acronym on first use; state assumptions, don't imply them.

**4 — Anticipate the objection in the text.**
Raise the counterargument yourself and answer it, rather than waiting for it to surface in discussion. If a reasonable colleague would push back, address it in the paragraph.

**5 — First person plural, present tense for decisions.**
"We recommend," "we propose" — not "it is recommended." State the decision being asked for explicitly, early.

**6 — Numbers support the argument; they don't replace it.**
Cite the figure, then explain what it means for the decision. A bare number asks the reader to interpret it themselves.

**7 — Moderate formality — write like the plainest-spoken smart person in the room.**
No jargon, but no over-explaining either; the reader is a peer. Contractions are fine. Closer to a well-argued essay than a legal document.

## Sounds like
> We recommend piloting the subscription tier in Q3 rather than waiting for the Q1 planning cycle. Our current model loses an estimated 12% of trial users in the first two weeks to a pricing question we could answer today. A three-month pilot with 200 users costs under $8K and tells us whether that number moves before we commit the 2027 roadmap to it.

## Apply this voice when
Drafting business plan sections for leadership, the board, or internal stakeholders — not sections going to a bank or outside investors.
