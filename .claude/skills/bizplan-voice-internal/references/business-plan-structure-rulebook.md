# Business Plan Structure Rulebook

An analysis of how a business plan should be ordered — the goal of each section, how it connects to the others, and what it needs to contain.

## Why order matters

A business plan is read in sequence by someone deciding whether to keep reading. Each section should answer the question the previous one raised, and set up the one that follows. Reordering sections breaks that chain of reasoning — that's why the order below isn't arbitrary.

---

## 1. Executive Summary

**Goal:** Give a reader who reads nothing else a complete picture — what the business does, why it matters, and what you're asking for.

**Relation to other sections:** A condensed preview of everything that follows. Write it last, even though it's read first.

**What to include:** One-line company description, the problem, the solution, market size, business model, traction or proof points, the ask (amount and use of funds), and the team's credibility in a sentence.

---

## 2. Problem / Opportunity

**Goal:** Establish that a real, sizeable problem exists before proposing a solution.

**Relation to other sections:** Sets up Solution — everything there should map back to a problem named here. Also feeds Market Analysis, since the problem defines who has it.

**What to include:** The specific pain point, who experiences it, how they cope today and why that's inadequate, evidence the problem is real (data, examples, quotes).

---

## 3. Solution / Product

**Goal:** Show how your product or service solves the problem just described.

**Relation to other sections:** Directly answers Problem. Sets up Business Model (how the solution is delivered and sold) and Competitive Landscape (why this beats the alternatives).

**What to include:** What the product is, how it works at a high level, why it's the right solution, current stage of development (concept, MVP, live).

---

## 4. Market Analysis

**Goal:** Prove the market is large enough — and defined narrowly enough — to build a business on.

**Relation to other sections:** Builds on Problem (who has it, how many). Feeds directly into Go-to-Market (how you'll reach this market) and Financial Plan (market size bounds revenue projections).

**What to include:** Target customer profile, total addressable market (TAM/SAM/SOM) with sourced figures, trends supporting growth.

---

## 5. Business Model

**Goal:** Explain exactly how the company makes money.

**Relation to other sections:** Follows from Solution (what you sell) and Market (who buys it). Feeds directly into Financial Plan — the model is the logic the numbers are built on.

**What to include:** Revenue streams, pricing structure, unit economics (cost to acquire/serve a customer vs. revenue per customer), sales cycle if relevant.

---

## 6. Go-to-Market Strategy

**Goal:** Show how the company will actually acquire the customers sized in Market Analysis.

**Relation to other sections:** Operationalizes Market Analysis and Business Model — turns "who will buy" into "how we will reach them."

**What to include:** Marketing channels, sales strategy or process, partnerships, launch sequence or phased rollout.

---

## 7. Competitive Landscape

**Goal:** Show you understand who else solves this problem, and why you win.

**Relation to other sections:** Tests the claims made in Solution — it should be clear why your solution beats named alternatives, not just that alternatives exist.

**What to include:** Named direct and indirect competitors, a comparison (a table works well), your specific differentiation or moat.

---

## 8. Team & Management

**Goal:** Convince the reader this specific group can execute everything described above.

**Relation to other sections:** Doesn't build directly on prior sections, but every claim made earlier — building the solution, entering the market, executing go-to-market — is only credible if this section backs it up.

**What to include:** Founders and key hires, relevant experience, gaps in the team and the plan to fill them, advisors or board if relevant.

---

## 9. Operations Plan

**Goal:** Explain how the business runs day-to-day at the scale described.

**Relation to other sections:** Grounds Business Model and Go-to-Market in practical execution.

**What to include:** Key operational processes, suppliers or partners, location/facilities, technology infrastructure. Can be short or omitted for simple service/software businesses.

---

## 10. Financial Plan

**Goal:** Translate every prior section into numbers — prove the business is financially viable.

**Relation to other sections:** The quantitative expression of Business Model, Market Analysis, and Go-to-Market. Every assumption here should trace back to a claim made earlier.

**What to include:** Revenue projections (3–5 years), cost structure, break-even analysis, cash flow, key assumptions stated explicitly.

---

## 11. Funding Request / Ask

**Goal:** State exactly what you want from the reader, and what it achieves.

**Relation to other sections:** Directly dependent on Financial Plan — the amount requested should map to a funding gap the numbers show.

**What to include:** Amount requested, use of funds (broken down), terms if applicable (equity, loan structure), milestones the funding will achieve.

---

## 12. Appendix

**Goal:** House supporting detail that would clutter the main narrative.

**Relation to other sections:** Referenced from, but not required to understand, any section above.

**What to include:** Detailed financial tables, resumes, product screenshots, letters of intent, patents, legal documents.

---

## Ordering notes

- This is the standard investor-facing order. For a bank loan or grant application, Financial Plan and repayment ability often need to move earlier.
- For an internal strategic plan, Team can move earlier (the reader already knows them) and Competitive Landscape can merge into Market Analysis.
- Whatever order is used, keep the underlying logic chain intact: Problem → Solution → Market → Model → Execution → Proof (team) → Numbers → Ask.
