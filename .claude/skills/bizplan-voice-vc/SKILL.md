# VC / Investor Business Plan Voice

Modeled on the three most-cited VC pitch frameworks (Sequoia Capital, Y Combinator, Guy Kawasaki), teardown analyses of Airbnb's original seed deck, and DocSend's data from 200,000+ investor deck views.

## How this voice writes
Built for a skim — investors spend under four minutes on a first read, so every section has to land fast. Confident and specific rather than hedged; optimistic in framing rather than risk-first, the opposite instinct from the bank voice — but every confident claim is backed by a real number, never a qualifier standing in for one.

## Rules

**1 — Lead with your strongest fact, not the expected order.**
Don't default to intro → problem → solution if something else is more compelling. Leading with a standard format instead of your strongest point is founders' most common structural mistake, per Y Combinator's fundraising guidance.

**2 — Pass the "anyone can understand it" test.**
State what the company does in one plain sentence, zero jargon. Airbnb's earliest pitch material described itself as letting people book rooms with locals instead of hotels — understandable in three seconds.

**3 — Show the proof; don't describe that it exists.**
The actual number, not the adjective. Not "gaining traction" — the booking count, the user count, the revenue figure.

**4 — Cut vague qualifiers entirely.**
No "very," "quite," "so," "good," "unique." Replace each with the number it's standing in for.

**5 — Avoid the phrases that read as inexperience.**
No "TAM is $100B, if we just get 1%," no "no competitors," no "viral" without a named mechanism, no "runway," no volunteering an "exit strategy."

**6 — State the business model in one sentence.**
How the company makes money is a single, complete sentence — not a menu of "potential revenue streams."

**7 — Three-beat narrative: problem, turn, proof.**
The customer's problem in their own words → the moment the solution appears → evidence it's already working.

**8 — Make the ask specific and falsifiable.**
"We're raising $[amount] to go from [metric] to [metric] by [date]." A specific ask forces a real decision; a vague one is easy to defer.

**9 — Back growth claims with a credible "why now."**
Market size alone isn't a claim — pair it with a stated reason the timing is right.

**10 — Keep the narrative positive; let the reader raise the risk.**
Don't volunteer downside scenarios in the main text. Address risk if asked, not before.

## Sounds like
> We're raising $1.2M to go from 40 to 400 paying customers by Q4 2027. We already convert 18% of trial signups without a sales team — the problem isn't demand, it's that we've only been in market for five months. Every dollar goes to two hires: one engineer to cut onboarding time in half, one salesperson to run the outbound motion we've proven works manually.

## Apply this voice when
Drafting business plan or pitch content for VCs, angel investors, or any venture-style funding conversation.
